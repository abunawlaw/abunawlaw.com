Why Cameroon, why common law

Cameroon is bijural. Common law applies in the Northwest and Southwest regions; civil law applies elsewhere. Above both sit the OHADA Uniform Acts, which govern company law, secured transactions, insolvency and commercial arbitration nationwide.

For an investor arriving from London, Lagos, Accra or Nairobi, that combination is unfamiliar. Documents are drafted to common-law expectations; the register, the courts and the security regime are OHADA. Our lawyers work on both sides of that line, in both working languages, without translation loss.

We also act for Cameroonian companies going the other way — into CEMAC and ECOWAS markets, and into common-law jurisdictions where local counsel needs a partner who understands what was agreed at home.

Corporate structuring and restructuring

We advise on how a business should be organised: the choice of vehicle, the allocation of shares and control, group structures and holding arrangements, and the changes that follow growth, investment or a change of ownership.

Restructuring work includes capital increases and reductions, conversions between company forms, share transfers, mergers and reorganisations within a group, and the amendments to articles and registrations each step requires.

Commercial transactions

Drafting, review and negotiation of the agreements a business runs on: supply, services and distribution contracts, commercial agency, licensing, shareholder and joint-venture agreements, and terms of business.

We draft to be enforced. Contracts are prepared in English, French or both, with governing-law and dispute-resolution provisions chosen for where the counterparty and its assets actually are.

Company incorporation and business formation

We take businesses from first decision to registration under the OHADA Uniform Act on Commercial Companies: choice of vehicle, articles of association, capital deposit, notarial steps, registration at the RCCM and the tax and social-security registrations that follow.

SA
Public company. Board or sole-administrator governance, statutory auditor, higher minimum capital. Suited to regulated businesses and ventures with institutional shareholders.
SARL
The default operating company. Manager-run, flexible capital, share transfers subject to statutory and contractual consent.
SAS
Contractual freedom in the articles: tailored share classes, veto rights and transfer mechanics.
GIE
Economic interest group for pooled activity between existing businesses.
Branch
Registration of a foreign company’s branch, with its statutory duration limits and conversion requirements.

Regulatory compliance advisory

Advice on the licences, authorisations and ongoing obligations that apply to a business in its sector, and on how to organise compliance so that it can be demonstrated to a regulator. We work with clients in banking and finance, payments, telecommunications, logistics, and oil and gas.

Corporate governance frameworks

Board and management structures, delegations of authority, internal rules, and the policies that allow a company to show how decisions are made and recorded. We review existing frameworks against the Uniform Act and the company’s own articles, and draft what is missing.

Tax compliance

Legal support on corporate tax obligations under the General Tax Code: registration, the tax consequences of structuring and transfers, withholding on payments, and responses to enquiries from the tax administration. We work alongside clients’ accountants and tax advisers.

AML compliance

Advice on anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing obligations under the CEMAC framework: customer due diligence, record-keeping, suspicious-transaction reporting, and the internal policies and training that regulated entities are expected to maintain.

Data protection

Advice on the collection, use, storage and transfer of personal data under Cameroonian law: privacy notices, consent, data-processing agreements with service providers, cross-border transfers, and internal data-handling policies for employees and customers.

NGO and organisational policy review

For non-governmental organisations, human resources firms and other organisations: review of constitutions, governance documents and internal policies — including conduct, safeguarding, procurement and staff policies — with recommendations and redrafts that strengthen accountability.

Questions we are asked

Which vehicle should a new business use in Cameroon?

Most new operations are a SARL, for its light governance, or an SAS where shareholders want tailored arrangements in the articles. A branch can suit a foreign company testing the market but is time-limited. The right answer depends on licensing, the shareholders and the planned exit.

Can our contracts be governed by English law?

Commercial contracts between sophisticated parties often can be. Company constitutions, employment relationships and interests in Cameroonian land remain subject to local and OHADA law, so we usually split the document suite rather than force one governing law across it.

Real Estate & Land Law

Land in Cameroon is governed by the 1974 Land Ordinances and a registration system that sits alongside living customary tenure. Most disputes we see begin with a title that was never properly verified.

The 1974 Land Ordinances and the land certificate

The 1974 Ordinances reorganised land into national lands, state private and public property, and privately held registered land. The land certificate — the titre foncier — is the state’s official record of ownership and the starting point of any transaction.

We obtain certificates, act on transfers and mutations, and verify existing certificates at the land registry: chain of title, area and boundaries as surveyed, registered charges, and whether the certificate has been the subject of a challenge or a parallel grant.

National lands, state property, grants and concessions

Land held by the state is allocated by temporary grant, long lease or concession, each with development conditions and a route to definitive title once those conditions are met. We advise applicants on the consultative-board process, on the conditions attached to a grant, and on what happens when a development programme slips behind the timetable in the instrument.

Customary tenure and registered title

In the Northwest and Southwest regions in particular, land is frequently occupied and transferred under customary arrangements that the register does not reflect. A valid certificate and a long-standing customary claim can point to the same parcel.

We investigate occupation on the ground, engage with families and traditional authorities, document consents and releases properly, and advise on how a court is likely to treat the competing positions. Buyers who skip this step usually pay for it later.

Acquisition due diligence, boundaries and competing claims

Registry searches, instruction of a licensed surveyor, reconciliation of the survey plan with the certificate, physical inspection for occupation and encroachment, enquiry into pending proceedings, and structuring of the purchase so that price is released against registration rather than signature.

Commercial and residential leases, ground leases

Commercial leases under the Uniform Act on General Commercial Law, including renewal rights, rent review and the compensation payable on refusal to renew; residential tenancies; and long ground leases for industrial and retail development, where the interaction between the lease term and the tenant’s capital programme needs to be set out precisely.

Development and construction; permitting

Development agreements, construction contracts and consultant appointments, performance security and retention, building and subdivision permits, environmental and social requirements, and the sequencing of approvals against funding drawdowns.

Mortgages and land-based security

Conventional and judicial mortgages over registered land, registration and ranking, security over leasehold interests and over land held under grant, releases on repayment, and enforcement by sale — including the practical obstacles enforcement meets where the land is occupied.

Compulsory acquisition and compensation

Acting for owners, occupiers and businesses affected by acquisition for public purposes: participating in the enquiry and valuation process, challenging the assessment of crops, structures and business losses, and pursuing payment where an award has been made but not settled.

Land disputes and litigation strategy

Competing certificates, boundary and encroachment claims, family and succession disputes over land, challenges to the issue of a certificate, and injunctions to stop works pending trial. We advise early on whether a case is better resolved by negotiated release and re-registration than by judgment.

Succession & Probate

We help families plan for succession and settle estates, including estates that include land and heirs living outside Cameroon.

Succession planning

Wills, the appointment of executors and guardians, and planning for family property and family businesses. Customary succession still governs many estates in practice and does not always produce the statutory result, particularly for spouses and children. We plan so that the question is settled in advance rather than argued after death.

Letters of administration

Where there is no will, we apply for letters of administration, identify the persons entitled to administer and inherit, collect and value the estate, and deal with land certificates and bank accounts held in the deceased’s name.

Probate matters

Applications for probate of a will, advice to executors on their duties, distribution to beneficiaries, and contested applications between family members. For heirs abroad we arrange local representation and coordinate with foreign proceedings.

Registering security at the RCCM: what lenders get wrong

By [author name, title]

Security taken over assets in Cameroon is only as good as its registration. The documents are rarely the problem; the register usually is.

The OHADA Uniform Act on Secured Transactions gives lenders a wide menu: pledges of shares, bank accounts, receivables, stock and equipment, assignments by way of security, and the guarantee and counter-guarantee regime. Most of these interests take effect against third parties only once they are entered in the Trade and Personal Property Credit Register — the RCCM — held at the registry of the competent court.

Registration is not a formality

Signing does not create priority. Where two lenders take security over the same asset, the order of entry in the register ordinarily decides which of them is paid first. A facility that closed on time but registered late can sit behind a competitor that documented the same collateral a month later.

Registrations also expire. A registered interest is entered for a period, and a renewal missed during a refinancing or a change of agent leaves the lender unsecured against third parties while the loan is still outstanding. In portfolio reviews this is the single most common defect we find.

Three practical points

  1. Make registration a condition subsequent with a hard date and evidence requirement, not a covenant to use reasonable endeavours.
  2. Keep a renewal calendar at agent level, not at borrower level, and test it at every amendment.
  3. Search the register before each drawdown of a revolving facility, not only at closing.

Enforcement

A properly registered interest opens routes that do not require a full trial on the merits. Those routes are procedural and unforgiving: the notices, time limits and proofs are prescribed, and a step taken out of order can restart the process. Lenders should plan enforcement while documenting the facility, when the borrower is still cooperative and the register is still clean.
